# **The Small Business AI Blind Spot**

## **Adoption is growing. Understanding and oversight need to catch up.**

**An XpressIQ report for small business owners and leaders**  
*28 October 2026*

### **Executive summary**

Artificial intelligence is becoming part of everyday business operations. Small firms use it to draft communications, analyse information, support customers and automate work. Increasingly, some are connecting AI to existing business systems and building workflows of their own.

That creates an opportunity: a small business can now access capabilities that once required a much larger team. It also creates a management challenge. An AI tool may be easy to switch on, but its effects depend on what information it can access, what decisions people make using its output, and whether it has permission to take actions.

The article that prompted this report puts the issue in a striking headline: “90% of small businesses are already using AI. Only 23% understand exactly what it is they have implemented.” **Those figures should not be repeated as established facts.** The article does not substantiate the 90% claim, and the 23% figure appears to refer to a narrower question about formal review processes—not to whether business owners understand their AI. [DEV Community](https://dev.to/devsdaddy/90-of-small-businesses-are-already-using-ai-only-23-understand-exactly-what-it-is-they-have-53d1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The underlying issue remains credible. Research suggests that some businesses are deploying or building AI faster than they are putting consistent oversight around it. The practical response is straightforward: identify where AI is used, understand the information and systems it can reach, assign responsibility, and apply controls proportionate to what it can do.

### **What the headline gets wrong**

The 23% figure can be traced to the **Q3 2026 Pax8 SMB AI Pulse Report**. Pax8 reports that formal, documented review of internally built AI ranged from 23% in the United Kingdom to 45% in Sweden.

That is a finding about **documented review**, not a measurement of how many business owners understand what they have implemented. A business may understand a tool but lack a formal review process; another may have a review process yet still misunderstand a particular risk. [Pax8](https://www.pax8.com/en-us/news-post/pax8-research-finds-small-businesses-are-building-ai-faster-than-they-can-govern-it/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The 90% claim is also unsupported in the article’s body. Estimates of AI adoption differ considerably depending on the businesses surveyed, the definition of AI, and whether the question covers an individual using a chatbot, AI embedded in software, or a system integrated into a business process.

For example, the UK government’s **2026 Business Data Survey** found that 51% of small businesses with 10–49 employees that handled digitised data reported using AI technologies. The corresponding figures were 41% for microbusinesses and 40% for sole traders. Those findings do not establish the true figure for every business under every definition, but they demonstrate why a universal 90% claim requires a clearly identified source and methodology. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/uk-business-data-survey-2026/uk-business-data-survey-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**A defensible alternative headline is:**

> **Small businesses are adopting and building AI, but oversight is struggling to keep pace.**

### **What the research does show**

Pax8 surveyed 1,150 business owners, executives, functional leaders and managers involved in technology decisions at organisations with 5–499 employees across the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany and Sweden. The UK sample contained 350 respondents.

Its results point to a more complex picture than the original headline suggests:

| Finding | What it means |
| ----- | ----- |
| **67% of AI-engaged businesses** had built at least one AI solution in regular use. | Some firms are adapting or creating solutions, not merely purchasing software. |
| **35% of AI-engaged businesses** had discovered AI tools, workflows or agents operating without official approval in the previous six months. | Leaders may not have a complete picture of AI activity. |
| **49% of businesses that paid for AI** had received at least one higher-than-expected bill or usage charge. | The cost of AI can be difficult to predict, particularly with usage-based services. |
| **55% of decision-makers** spent three or more hours a week reviewing, editing or fact-checking AI-generated work. | Time saved by AI can be partly offset by the work required to check it. |

These percentages have different denominators and should not be treated as findings about all small businesses. Pax8 also notes that smaller subgroups should be interpreted as directional. Its sample includes medium-sized organisations and excludes firms with fewer than five employees. Pax8 is a commercial AI and cloud marketplace, so its research should also be read alongside independent sources. [Pax8](https://www.pax8.com/en-us/news-post/pax8-research-finds-small-businesses-are-building-ai-faster-than-they-can-govern-it/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The OECD reaches a complementary conclusion in its 2026 survey: uptake of off-the-shelf AI is increasing, while strategic, targeted and secure integration remains uneven. The OECD also notes that its sample of more than 2,000 SMEs across 12 countries is **not representative**, so it is best used to understand patterns and barriers rather than to estimate universal adoption rates. [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/empowering-smes-in-the-age-of-ai_bf5a9816-en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### **The real business issue: visibility**

For many owners, the problem is not a lack of interest in AI. It is a lack of visibility into how it has entered the business.

AI may be present in a tool that an employee deliberately chose, or as a feature added to software the company already uses. A marketing platform may generate campaign copy. A meeting application may produce summaries. A customer management system may score leads. An automation may read a form, draft a response and update a record.

Each example carries a different level of risk. The owner does not need to understand the underlying mathematics of an AI model. They do need clear answers to a few operational questions:

1. **Where is AI being used?**  
2. **What work is it doing, and what benefit is expected?**  
3. **What business or personal information can it access?**  
4. **Can it only make suggestions, or can it take action?**  
5. **Who checks its work and owns the result?**  
6. **What does it cost, and how can it be stopped?**

If those answers are unclear, a useful first step is an inventory—not a company-wide ban.

### **Three levels of AI use**

A practical way to understand AI risk is to look at the degree of access and authority a system has.

| Level | Typical example | Main management question |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Assistance** | A person asks AI to draft an email or summarise a document. | Is the information appropriate to enter, and is the output checked? |
| **Connected workflow** | AI reads customer enquiries and prepares updates in a CRM. | Which records can it access or change, and who reviews the result? |
| **Action-taking agent** | AI can send messages, update records or trigger transactions through connected tools. | What is it authorised to do, what requires approval, and how is activity monitored? |

The consequences tend to increase as access and autonomy increase. A mistaken internal draft can usually be corrected before it is used. A system that can change records, communicate with customers or initiate transactions needs stronger safeguards.

The UK National Cyber Security Centre advises organisations to define what an agent should achieve, what actions it may take, and when it must stop for human approval. It also cautions against relying on instructions to the model alone as a security control. [National Cyber Security Centre](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/managing-the-cyber-risk-of-agentic-ai?trk=public_post_comment-text&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### **Five risks owners should understand**

#### **1\. Confidential information may travel further than expected**

Employees may enter customer records, financial details, contracts or personnel information into an AI tool without knowing how the provider stores or uses it.

The question is not simply whether a tool is popular or useful. It is whether the organisation understands what data is being shared, whether that sharing is appropriate, and what settings and contractual terms apply.

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office recommends limiting an agent’s access to the personal information needed for its stated purpose. It also identifies access permissions and human approval as potential controls. [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/research-reports-impact-and-evaluation/research-and-reports/technology-and-innovation/tech-horizons-and-ico-tech-futures/ico-tech-futures-agentic-ai/data-protection-and-privacy-risks?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

#### **2\. Confident output can still be wrong**

AI can produce plausible answers that contain errors, omissions or unsupported claims. The risk is higher when an employee assumes a polished answer is a verified one.

Businesses should identify outputs where mistakes would matter most—such as financial information, customer commitments, legal content or advice—and establish who checks them before they are relied upon.

#### **3\. AI can be given too much access**

An assistant that can read a limited set of records is different from an agent with broad access to customer data, email, financial software and administrative settings.

Give each tool the minimum access it needs for the job. Where an action could create a material commitment, expose sensitive data or move money, require a meaningful human approval before it happens.

#### **4\. Useful experiments can become unmanaged processes**

An employee may create a helpful automation to save time. Months later, the business may depend on it even though no one has formally taken ownership, documented its connections or planned what happens if it fails.

This is one reason the Pax8 finding on unapproved AI matters. It does not prove every unapproved tool is dangerous, but it does suggest that leaders may be responsible for systems they do not know exist. [Pax8](https://www.pax8.com/en-us/news-post/pax8-research-finds-small-businesses-are-building-ai-faster-than-they-can-govern-it/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

#### **5\. Apparent savings can hide new costs**

A subscription fee is only one part of the cost. A realistic assessment should also consider usage charges, setup, maintenance, staff training, checking time and correcting mistakes.

The right measure is not simply “How much time did AI save?” It is:

> **What value did the complete process produce after checking, correction, maintenance and cost?**

### **A practical 30-day action plan**

A small business does not need a large governance department to begin. It needs a short, repeatable process.

#### **Week 1: Find out what is being used**

Ask staff which AI tools and features they use, including tools embedded in existing software. Make it clear that the purpose is to understand and improve the work. Record the tool, its purpose, its users and its approximate cost.

#### **Week 2: Map information and permissions**

For each tool, record what information goes into it, what systems it connects to and whether it can read, create, change, send or delete anything. Identify tools with access to personal data, financial information or customer communications.

#### **Week 3: Assign owners and apply proportionate controls**

Name one accountable person for each significant use. Remove access that is not needed. Set approval points for consequential actions, and agree how to pause or disconnect an automation if something goes wrong.

#### **Week 4: Review value and set simple rules**

For each use, ask whether it is saving meaningful time, improving quality or helping the business serve customers. Compare the benefit with the cost and checking effort. Then write a short policy explaining:

* Which tools are approved and how to request a new one.  
* What information staff may and may not enter.  
* Which outputs require review.  
* Which actions require human approval.  
* Who to contact if something goes wrong.

This approach aligns with the NCSC’s broader guidance that security should be considered across the design, deployment, operation and maintenance of AI systems. [National Cyber Security Centre](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/guidelines-secure-ai-system-development?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### **Questions every owner should ask a supplier**

Before adopting an AI feature or connecting it to a business system, ask the provider:

1. What data does the tool receive, store and share?  
2. Is our data used to train or improve models, and can that setting be controlled?  
3. What systems and records can the tool access?  
4. Can it take actions, and can we limit those actions?  
5. Are activity logs available to us?  
6. What happens if the tool makes a mistake or experiences a security incident?  
7. How are charges calculated, and can we set spending limits?  
8. How do we disable the feature, remove access and retrieve or delete our data?

The answers should be checked against the actual product settings and contract. A general sales assurance is less useful than a clear explanation of how the specific service is configured.

### **The opportunity for small businesses**

AI can help small firms make better use of limited time and expertise. It can support research, improve first drafts, reduce repetitive administration and make information easier to find. Connected workflows can go further, helping teams move work between systems and respond more consistently.

The commercial advantage comes from choosing a worthwhile problem, measuring the result and maintaining enough control to use the system with confidence. A business does not need to automate everything. It needs to know which activities benefit from AI, where human judgement remains essential, and whether the overall process is delivering value.

The strongest starting question for an owner is therefore:

> **Can we identify the AI working in our business, explain what each system can access and do, and name the person responsible for it?**

If the answer is no, that is not a reason to abandon AI. It is a reason to make the next improvement visible, manageable and measurable.
